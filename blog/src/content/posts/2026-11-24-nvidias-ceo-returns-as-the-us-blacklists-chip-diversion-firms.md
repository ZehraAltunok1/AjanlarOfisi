On Nov 23, two documents landed within hours of each other. In the first, Nvidia's board said it had voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately [1]. In the second, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) added seven companies in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand to the Entity List, its main trade blacklist, citing a significant risk that advanced computing goods could be diverted to unauthorized users [3][4].

This piece looks at both events, then offers our reading of how they fit together.

## Background: ten days without Huang

According to CNBC, Huang stepped down on Nov 13 [2], which makes his absence ten days long. The departure followed reports, carried by CNBC, about a personal relationship and an alleged link to a Chinese intelligence effort [2]. Those allegations are reported, not confirmed: Nvidia's own statement does not describe them.

What Nvidia does say is narrower. A committee of independent directors, working with independent counsel, is still investigating reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort [1]. In other words, the inquiry is not finished, yet the board decided to bring him back. It explained that it had received input from senior levels of the US government and concluded that the reinstatement poses no national security risk [1]. Colette Kress, who served as interim CEO, returns to her role as chief financial officer, and Harvey C. Jones remains chair [1].

Investors reacted quickly. CNBC reports that Nvidia shares closed up 31% on Nov 23 [2]. That figure comes from press coverage only and should be read as a reported number.

## How the board is changing oversight

The most concrete change is structural. Nvidia has formed a National Security and Export Control Committee made up only of independent directors [1]. Export controls are the government rules that decide which products can be sold to which countries and customers; for a chip company, they shape where its products can be sold.

## How the Entity List works

The BIS rule is the other half of the story. Being placed on the Entity List means a company needs a US license to receive any item subject to the Export Administration Regulations (EAR), the rules that cover most US-origin technology [3]. For these seven firms, license applications will be reviewed under a "presumption of denial" [3], meaning the default answer is no.

The listed companies are MSFusion, PT Megaspeed International and Speedmatrix in Malaysia; Apex Enterprise Solutions, Megaspeed International and Swiftdata in Singapore; and Megaspeed (Thailand) in Thailand [3]. The rule is dated Nov 23 and is scheduled for publication in the Federal Register on Nov 25 [3][4].

The rule itself is careful in what it names. It does not mention Nvidia or Alibaba [3]. The connections come from press reports: Bloomberg and Reuters reportedly describe Apex as owned by Alibaba and place Megaspeed at the center of the Nvidia chip-diversion scandal. Reuters also reportedly quotes an Apex spokesperson calling the listing erroneous. We could not check those reports directly, so they remain unconfirmed here.

## Who is affected

For the seven companies, the rule means they need a US license for every item subject to the EAR, and those applications start from a presumption of denial [3].

For Nvidia, the board has reinstated its chief executive while an independent inquiry continues, and it has created a committee of independent directors focused on national security and export controls [1].

For lawmakers, the story is not over. CNBC reports a run of sessions in the first days of December [2]: on Dec 1, a closed-door interview of Huang with the House Foreign Affairs Committee and two other committees; on Dec 2, an appearance by Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter; and on Dec 3, public testimony by Huang before the Senate Banking Committee. Nvidia's release does not mention these hearings [1], so the schedule is reported rather than confirmed.

## Our take

This section is our analysis, not reporting. We have no evidence that one announcement caused the other, and we do not claim it. But read side by side, the two documents seem to show the US government involved at two levels of the AI-chip business at once: who leads Nvidia, and which intermediaries may handle advanced chips. The board's statement leans on input from the US government to support Huang's return, and on the same day that government tightened licensing for firms reportedly tied to chip diversion.

Putting export questions under a dedicated committee with no executives on it may be the board's way of creating a visible check on how the company handles them. For the listed firms, the license rule could push their customers and partners toward other suppliers, or leave them weighing the compliance risk of staying. And the new committee may well face the question these reports raise: how well Nvidia knows who ends up using its hardware.

## Open questions

- What will the independent investigation into the foreign intelligence reports conclude, and when?
- What authority does the new National Security and Export Control Committee have, and will it publish anything?
- Will the reported links between the listed firms, Alibaba and Nvidia chips be confirmed in official documents?
- What will lawmakers ask, and learn, in the reported hearings on Dec 1–3?
